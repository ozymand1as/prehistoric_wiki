# Agriculture/Quinoa-Domestication

---
**Period:** Neolithic
**Region(s):** Andes (South America)
**Culture(s):** Inca, Tiwanaku, pre-Inca Andean cultures
**Time Range:** ~5,000–7,000 years ago
**Confidence:** High
**Evidence Type:** Material

## Overview
Quinoa (*Chenopodium quinoa*) is a pseudocereal domesticated in the Andes approximately 5,000–7,000 years ago. Originating in the Lake Titicaca basin of modern-day Peru and Bolivia, it became a staple food of the Inca Empire and remains a globally significant crop today.

## History & Development
- Quinoa was first domesticated in the Lake Titicaca basin by pre-Inca Andean cultures roughly 5,000–7,000 years ago.
- The Tiwanaku civilization cultivated quinoa as a primary crop in the high-altitude Altiplano region.
- The Inca Empire elevated quinoa to a sacred status, referring to it as *chisaya mama* ("mother of all grains").
- Inca agricultural engineers developed terrace farming and storage techniques to support large-scale quinoa production.
- Following the Spanish conquest, quinoa cultivation declined in favor of European crops but persisted in remote Andean communities.
- Modern global cultivation has expanded significantly, driven by demand for its nutritional properties.

## Function & Use
- **Primary Use:** Staple food source; grain-like seed used for human consumption
- **Secondary Uses:** Animal feed, medicinal applications, ritual offerings
- **Cultural Significance:** Revered by the Inca as a sacred crop; central to religious ceremonies and agricultural festivals; *chisaya mama* ("mother of all grains")

## Construction & Technique
- **Threshing:** Mature quinoa stalks were cut and dried, then beaten to separate seeds from chaff.
- **Winnowing:** Threshed seed was tossed in the air to separate lightweight chaff from heavier seeds using wind.
- **Saponin Removal:** The natural saponin coating on quinoa seeds produces a bitter taste; seeds were washed, soaked, or fermented to remove this coating before consumption.
- Processing tools included stone mortars, grinding stones, and woven winnowing baskets.

## Regional Variations
- **Lake Titicaca Basin (Peru/Bolivia):** Original domestication center; highest diversity of wild and cultivated quinoa varieties.
- **Altiplano Region:** High-altitude cultivation adapted to harsh, arid conditions with poor soil.
- **Coastal Peru:** Lower-altitude variants with different seed characteristics; introduced through Inca trade networks.
- **Modern Global Cultivation:** Expanded to North America, Europe, and Asia; bred for uniformity and commercial yield.

## Uncertainties & Research Questions
- The precise timeline of initial domestication remains debated; archaeological evidence is limited.
- The degree to which wild *Chenopodium* species contributed to quinoa's genetic diversity is unclear.
- How saponin removal techniques varied across different Andean cultures is not well documented.
