# Agriculture/Maize-Agriculture

---
**Period:** Neolithic / Pre-Columbian / Post-Contact
**Region(s):** Mesoamerica, Americas, Europe, Africa, Asia
**Culture(s):** Olmec, Maya, Aztec, Zapotec, various Indigenous peoples of the Americas
**Time Range:** ~10,000 BCE – present
**Confidence:** High
**Evidence Type:** Material / Archaeological / Ethnographic

## Overview
Maize (corn) is one of the most important domesticated crops in human history, originating from the wild grass teosinte in Mesoamerica approximately 10,000 years ago. It became a staple food across the Americas and, after 1492 CE, spread to Europe, Africa, and Asia, transforming global agriculture and diets.

## History & Development
- Maize domestication from teosinte began in Mesoamerica ~10,000 years ago
- Earliest archaeological evidence comes from Guilá Naquitz cave, Oaxaca, Mexico (~4300 BCE)
- Maize cultivation expanded across diverse climates in the Americas, with numerous varieties developed
- It became a key factor in the development of complex societies, including the Olmec, Maya, and Aztec civilizations
- After 1492 CE, maize was introduced to Europe, Africa, and Asia through the Columbian Exchange

## Function & Use
- **Primary Use:** Staple food crop (grain for human consumption)
- **Secondary Uses:** Animal feed, industrial product (cornmeal, corn oil, ethanol), ceremonial offerings
- **Cultural Significance:** Central to Mesoamerican cosmology and mythology; a foundational crop in the Three Sisters companion planting system; integral to rituals and social organization among Indigenous peoples of the Americas

## Construction & Technique
- Maize was cultivated through selective breeding of teosinte over thousands of years
- Processing techniques included nixtamalization — treating dried maize with lime (calcium hydroxide) or wood ash to remove the hull and improve nutritional value
- Nixtamalization increases the bioavailability of niacin and calcium and improves dough texture for making tortillas, tamales, and other staple foods
- Cultivation techniques varied by region but commonly included mound planting and intercropping

## Regional Variations
- **Mesoamerica:** Diverse landrace varieties adapted to tropical and highland climates; basis for Maya and Aztec agriculture
- **North America:** Varieties adapted to shorter growing seasons; Three Sisters polyculture system (maize, beans, squash)
- **South America:** Highland and lowland varieties; incorporated into Andean agricultural systems
- **Post-1492 Global:** Thousands of modern cultivars developed worldwide, adapted to temperate, tropical, and arid climates

## Uncertainties & Research Questions
- The exact timeline and geographic origin of the earliest domestication events remain debated
- The degree to which teosinte intermediates were involved in the domestication process is still under investigation
- How nixtamalization spread across different cultures and its independent invention in various regions
