# Crop Domestication

---
**Period:** Neolithic Revolution (First Agricultural Revolution)
**Region(s):** Global (11+ centers of origin)
**Culture(s):** Neolithic cultures worldwide
**Time Range:** 105,000 BCE - 2,000 BCE
**Confidence:** High
**Evidence Type:** Material, Written, Inferred

## Overview
Crop domestication was a slow process preceded by centuries/millennia of pre-domestication cultivation (harvesting wild stands before planting them). Wild grains were collected from at least 105,000 years ago; the earliest known cultivation of cereals (emmer, barley, oats) dates to ~23,000 years ago near the Sea of Galilee (Paleolithic Levant). The transition from foraging to farming involved initial intensification and sedentism → wild stands being planted → gradual domestication. Domestication increased yield, improved disease resistance and drought tolerance, eased harvest, and improved taste/nutritional value.

## History & Development

### The Process of Domestication
Domestication was a slow process preceded by centuries/millennia of pre-domestication cultivation. Early farmers initially selected plants with desirable characteristics to use as seeds for future generations. Over time, this evolved from simple selection to deliberate hybridization.

**Pre-domestication Cultivation:**
- Wild grains were collected from at least 105,000 years ago
- The earliest known cultivation of cereals (emmer, barley, oats) dates to ~23,000 years ago near the Sea of Galilee (Paleolithic Levant)
- The transition from foraging to farming involved: initial intensification and sedentism → wild stands being planted → gradual domestication

**Benefits of Domestication:**
- Increased yield
- Improved disease resistance and drought tolerance
- Eased harvest
- Improved taste and nutritional value

### Domestication of Plants in Different Regions

**West Asia (Fertile Crescent) ~10,000–9,000 years ago:**
- Cereals: einkorn wheat, emmer wheat, barley
- Legumes: lentil, pea, bitter vetch, chickpea
- Flax
- Sheep domesticated ~13,000–11,000 years ago
- Cattle domesticated ~10,500 years ago
- Pigs domesticated ~10,500 years ago

**East Asia:**
- Rice domesticated 11,500–6,200 BC in China (earliest cultivation ~5,700 BC)
- Also mung, soy, azuki beans
- Yangtze River basin rice + paddy field cultivation 13,500–8,200 years ago
- Broomcorn millet (East Asia)

**Africa — Three Independent Centers:**
- Ethiopian Highlands: coffee, khat, ensete, noog, teff, finger millet
- Sahel: sorghum, pearl millet (~7,000 years ago)
- West Africa: African rice, yams, oil palm, kola nut
- Nile Valley agriculture related to migration from Fertile Crescent

**Americas:**
- Mesoamerica: maize (from teosinte ~10,000–6,000 years ago), squash (~6,000 BCE), beans (≤4,000 BCE), cacao, turkey
- South America: potato (10,000–7,000 years ago), manioc, beans, coca, llama, alpaca, guinea pig, cotton (~5,600 years ago)
- North America: sunflower, tobacco, squash, Chenopodium (~2,500 BCE)
- Three Sisters companion planting (winter squash, maize, climbing beans)

**New Guinea:**
- Taro (~11,000 BP, earliest cultivated crop in the world at ~28,000 years ago in Solomon Islands)
- Yam, banana, sugarcane (~9,000 years ago)
- Kuk Swamp evidence of drainage ditches and cultivation

**Southeast Asia / Austronesia:**
- Rice cultivation technology spread via Austronesian migrations
- Banana, pepper acquired from Papuan and Dravidian-speaking regions

### Early Agricultural Practices

**Irrigation:** Traced as far back as 6th millennium BCE in Khuzistan (Iran); Sumerians (~8000 BC) relied on Tigris/Euphrates rivers and canal systems; Ancient Egypt relied on Nile flooding.

**Tillage:** Breaking up soil with tools (plow, harrow) to prepare for planting; ranges from conventional to no-till.

**Slash-and-burn:** Used by indigenous peoples (e.g., fire-stick farming in Australia, forest gardening in Pacific Northwest).

**Terrace farming:** Aztecs formed terraced hillsides; Andean civilizations.

**Chinampas:** Aztec artificial islands for agriculture.

**Fallow:** Used in Great Plains to conserve soil moisture.

**Paddy field cultivation:** Developed in China with rice.

**Crop rotation, intercropping, companion planting (Three Sisters).**

**Granaries:** Developed for storing surplus grain; earliest cheese-making ~5,500 BCE in Poland.

### Agricultural Technologies

**Ploughs:** Appear in pictographs ~3000 BC (Mesopotamia); animal-drawn plough in Indus Valley ~2500 BC; heavy ploughs with iron ploughshares and mouldboards in China by late 2nd century BC, spreading westward across Eurasia.

**Irrigation:** Sumerian canal systems (~8000 BC); Egyptian Nile flooding; Indus Valley irrigation; Chinese irrigation by 1st century BC; modern center-pivot irrigation.

**Water management:** Rain-fed agriculture in temperate zones; irrigation essential in subtropical/arid environments (agriculture = 70% of global freshwater use).

**Mechanization:** Motorized mechanization increased significantly; Sub-Saharan Africa is the only region where adoption has stalled.

**Fertilizers:** Synthetic fertilizers in landless livestock systems; composting, cover crops in traditional systems.

**Pest control:** Chemical, biological, mechanical (tillage), cultural practices (crop rotation, culling, cover crops, intercropping).

## Function & Use
- **Primary Use:** Food production through cultivated crops
- **Secondary Uses:** Seed supply for future planting, surplus storage, trade goods, cultural/ritual significance
- **Cultural Significance:** Companion planting (Three Sisters) reflects ecological knowledge; granaries and storage systems enabled settled life; agricultural practices shaped social organization and trade networks

## Construction & Technique
- **Pre-domestication cultivation:** Harvesting wild stands before planting them; centuries/millennia before full domestication
- **Selection:** Early farmers selected plants with desirable characteristics (larger yield, better taste, disease resistance)
- **Hybridization:** Over time, evolved from simple selection to deliberate hybridization
- **Cultivation techniques:** Irrigation systems, tillage methods, terrace construction, chinampa building, paddy field drainage
- **Storage:** Granaries for surplus grain; cheese-making ~5,500 BCE in Poland
- **Processing:** Tools for harvesting, threshing, grinding, and cooking crops

## Regional Variations
- **West Asia (Fertile Crescent):** Founder crops — emmer wheat, einkorn wheat, barley, lentils, peas, chickpeas, bitter vetch, flax; sheep ~13,000–11,000 years ago; cattle/pigs ~10,500 years ago
- **East Asia (China):** Rice (11,500–6,200 BC), mung, soy, azuki beans, broomcorn millet; paddy field cultivation 13,500–8,200 years ago
- **Africa (Ethiopian Highlands):** Coffee, khat, ensete, noog, teff, finger millet; Sahel: sorghum, pearl millet; West Africa: African rice, yams, oil palm, kola nut
- **Mesoamerica:** Maize (from teosinte), squash, beans, cacao, turkey; Three Sisters companion planting
- **South America:** Potato, manioc, beans, coca, llama, alpaca, guinea pig, cotton
- **North America:** Sunflower, tobacco, squash, Chenopodium
- **New Guinea:** Taro, yam, banana, sugarcane; Kuk Swamp drainage ditches and cultivation
- **Southeast Asia / Austronesia:** Rice cultivation via Austronesian migrations; banana, pepper from Papuan and Dravidian-speaking regions

## Uncertainties & Research Questions
- Reasons why human populations adopted agriculture roughly 12,000 years ago remain "imperfectly understood"
- Exact timing of plant vs. animal domestication in different regions is uncertain
- How early experimental gardening was used and its functional significance unclear
- Causality: Whether agriculture preceded politics or vice versa remains debated
- Whether introduction of agriculture by already existing political organisation was necessary or not
- The East-West axis hypothesis requires further testing with genetic and archaeological data
- Health trade-offs of agriculture vs. foraging need more detailed comparative analysis
