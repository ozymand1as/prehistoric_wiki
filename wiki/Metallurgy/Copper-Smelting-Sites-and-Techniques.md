## Overview

Copper smelting is the process of applying heat and a chemical reducing agent to copper ore to extract the base metal, marking the transition from the Copper Age (Chalcolithic) to the Bronze Age. It was a critical technological development that revolutionized tool-making, warfare, and trade networks across the ancient world. Copper was the first metal smelted, with evidence dating back to the 5th millennium BC in the Balkans.
## History & Development

Smelting uses heat and a chemical reducing agent to decompose the ore, driving off other elements as gases or slag and leaving the metal behind. The reducing agent is commonly a fossil-fuel source of carbon, such as carbon monoxide from incomplete combustion of charcoal. The oxygen in the ore binds to carbon at high temperatures, as the chemical potential energy of the bonds in carbon dioxide is lower than that of the bonds in the ore. [2]

The earliest securely dated copper smelting evidence comes from Belovode, Serbia (5500–5000 BC), with Pločnik, Serbia (~5000 BC) providing the oldest securely dated high-temperature copper making. Rudna Glava, Serbia also records earlier copper smelting from the 5th millennium BC. The oldest copper use is evidenced by copper beads from Shanidar Cave, Iraq (~8700 BC), while Çayönü Tepesi in Eastern Anatolia (7200–6600 BC) shows the earliest cold-hammering of native copper.

Copper was the first metal smelted, though campfires fall approximately 200°C short of the temperatures required, suggesting pottery kilns may have been the first smelting vessels. Sulfide ores such as chalcopyrite (CuFeS₂) were roasted before smelting in order to convert the sulfides to oxides, which are more readily reduced to the metal. Roasting heats the ore in the presence of oxygen from air, oxidizing the ore and liberating the sulfur as sulfur dioxide gas. Limestone or dolomite was added as a flux to bind impurities into slag, which floats on the molten matte allowing separation.

The Copper Age (Chalcolithic) gave way to the Bronze Age when the copper-tin alloy was developed around 3500 BC in Asia Minor. Arsenical bronze was also independently developed in multiple regions.

The smelting process proceeds through several stages: Concentration → Roasting → Smelting (matte) → Converting → Fire refining → Electrolytic refining. [3]

## Function & Use

- **Primary Use:** Production of durable metal tools, weapons, and ornaments from smelted copper and bronze
- **Secondary Uses:** Trade commodity, ritual objects, status symbols, currency in early economies [4]
- **Cultural Significance:** Copper smelting marked the transition from the Stone Age to the Copper Age and then the Bronze Age, fundamentally reshaping societies, warfare, and trade networks across the ancient world

## Construction & Technique

- Ore was mined and transported to smelting sites
- Sulfide ores (e.g., chalcopyrite CuFeS₂) were roasted in air to convert them to oxides, liberating SO₂ [5]
- Roasting heated the ore in the presence of oxygen from air, oxidizing the ore
- The ore was then smelted using heat and a reducing agent (charcoal producing CO)
- Charcoal was the reducing agent in prehistoric times; carbon monoxide removes oxygen from ore at high temperatures
- Fluxes such as limestone or dolomite were added to bind impurities into slag
- Slag floats on molten matte, allowing separation of metal from impurities [6]
- Smelting requires temperatures above 1085°C (copper melting point); pottery kilns (~900°C) were insufficient alone, suggesting purpose-built furnaces were necessary
- The Americas developed metallurgy independently but never progressed to iron smelting before European contact

## Regional Variations

- **Europe/Balkans:** Serbian sites (Belovode, Pločnik, Rudna Glava) represent the earliest European smelting evidence; Anatolia (Turkey) was a key early center with Çayönü Tepesi showing earliest cold-hammering of native copper (7200–6600 BC) [7]
- **Middle East:** Timna Valley, Israel — continuous copper mining from the 5th millennium BC (4th millennium BC copper mines, one of the world's oldest); Çatalhöyük — early lead/cupellation attempts; Shanidar Cave, Iraq — oldest copper beads (~8700 BC)
- **Asia:** China — blast furnace technology by 200 BC (Qin dynasty); bronze ritual casting (Shang/Zhou dynasties); India — significant later smelting capacity
- **Africa:** Lejja, Nigeria — iron smelting pre-2000 BC; East Africa — carbon steel production ~1st century AD; no significant copper smelting evidence pre-colonial
- **Americas:** Andes — Moche culture (200 BC–600 CE) adobe brick furnaces with 3+ blow pipes; Puma Punku, Bolivia (800–500 BC) portable smelting kilns for I-shaped cramps; Inca Empire — tin bronze, arsenical bronze, arsenical copper; Qullasuyu (Chile) major Inca metallurgy center; North America — Old Copper Culture (Great Lakes, ~6500 BC) cold hammering only, no smelting; Mississippian culture (c. 1200 CE) copper sheet working via hammering and annealing at Cahokia; Mesoamerica — metallurgy appeared ~800 CE via contact with South America, lost-wax casting, bronze axes and bells by 1300–1530 CE

## Uncertainties & Research Questions

- The exact nature of many prehistoric smelting techniques remains unknown due to lack of preservation
- The transition from pottery kilns to purpose-built smelting furnaces requires further archaeological evidence
- Cross-regional comparisons of smelting technologies need more detailed analysis
- The independent development of metallurgy in the Americas versus potential diffusion routes remain debated
- Ethnographic parallels need verification
