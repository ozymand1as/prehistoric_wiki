# Corbelling

Corbelling is a construction technique where each successive course of masonry projects inward beyond the course below, creating a self-supporting arch, vault, or dome without centering. It is one of the oldest methods for spanning spaces in stone. [1]

## Overview

Corbelling appears independently in multiple regions where stone is abundant and timber for lintels is scarce. It requires precise geometry to maintain stability but can create impressive spans without formwork.

## Principle

### Geometry
- **Projection**: Each course extends inward beyond the one below [2]
- **Angle**: Typically 5-15° from vertical
- **Stability**: Center of gravity must remain within base
- **Keystone**: Final slab closes the opening

### Forces
- **Compression only**: Stone strong in compression [3]
- **No tension**: Unlike true arches
- **Thrust**: Outward at base, requires buttressing

## Major Traditions

### Mycenaean Tholos Tombs (Greece, ~1,500–1,200 BCE)
- **Treasury of Atreus**: 14.5 m diameter, 13 m high
- **Precision**: Courses leveled, uniform projection
- **Keystone**: Single slab, removable
- **Dromos**: Stone-lined entrance passage

### Nuragic Towers (Sardinia, ~1,500–500 BCE)
- **Nuraghi**: 7,000+ towers, central chamber corbelled
- **Complexes**: Multiple towers, courtyards, walls
- **Construction**: Rough stone, no mortar
- **Function**: Defense, habitation, ritual

### Neolithic Orkney (Scotland, ~3,000 BCE)
- **Maeshowe**: Chambered cairn, corbelled chamber
- **Skara Brae**: Corbelled cells in houses
- **Stones**: Local flagstone, precise fitting

### Ancestral Pueblo (US Southwest, ~900–1,300 CE)
- **Kivas**: Circular, corbelled roofs
- **Great Kivas**: Community ceremonial spaces
- **Construction**: Sandstone, mud mortar

### Mycenaean Fortifications
- **Cyclopean walls**: Not corbelled but related
- **Posterns**: Corbelled gateways (Tiryns, Mycenae)
- **Galleries**: Corbelled passages in walls

## Construction Technique

### Laying Courses
1. **Base course**: Level, solid foundation
2. **Projection**: Each stone projects 5-15 cm
3. **Leveling**: Each course leveled with shims
4. **Overlap**: Stones overlap vertical joints
5. **Keystone**: Final piece locks dome

### Materials
- **Stone**: Limestone, sandstone, basalt
- **Mortar**: Often none (dry stone), sometimes clay
- **Shims**: Stone chips, pottery for leveling

## Engineering Limits

| Parameter | Typical Range |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Span** | 5-15 m (domes) |
| **Height** | Up to span radius |
| **Projection per course** | 5-15 cm |
| **Courses** | 20-40 |
| **Stone weight** | 50-500 kg |

## Advantages & Limitations

| Advantage | Limitation |
|-----------|------------|
| No centering/formwork | Limited span vs true arch |
| Local stone only | Heavy, thick walls needed |
| Durable, fireproof | Requires skilled masons |
| Compression only | Outward thrust at base |
