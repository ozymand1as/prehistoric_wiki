# European Prehistoric Housing

European housing traditions show a clear evolutionary sequence from Paleolithic cave occupation through Neolithic longhouses to Bronze Age byre-houses and Iron Age roundhouses, with exceptional preservation in Alpine lake dwellings and British hillforts.

## Overview

Europe's diverse environments—from Mediterranean to Arctic, Atlantic to steppe—produced regionally distinct but interconnected architectural traditions. The continent provides the world's best-documented prehistoric housing sequence due to extensive excavation, dendrochronology, and experimental archaeology.

## Paleolithic (40,000–12,000 BCE)

### Cave & Rock Shelter Occupation
- **Sites**: Lascaux, Chauvet, Dolní Věstonice, Mezhirich
- **Modification**: Hearths, windbreaks, spatial zoning
- **Mammoth bone huts**: Ukraine, Russia, Czech Republic

### Open-Air Shelters
- **Lean-tos, conical tents**: Hide-covered pole frames
- **Semi-subterranean**: Pit dwellings with pole superstructure

## Mesolithic (12,000–6,000 BCE)

### Post-Built Huts
- **Howick House** (UK, 7,600 BCE): Oldest British house
- **Star Carr** (UK, 9,000 BCE): Platform dwellings
- **Mount Sandel** (Ireland): Circular post-ring

### Seasonal Camps
- **Coastal**: Shell middens, lean-tos
- **Inland**: Hunting/fishing logistical camps

## Neolithic (6,000–2,500 BCE)

### LBK Longhouses (Central/Western Europe)
- **Three-aisled**: Central nave + side aisles
- **Dimensions**: 20-45 m × 6-7 m
- **Construction**: Oak posts, wattle-daub, thatch
- **Settlements**: 5-15 houses, palisades

### Alpine Pile Dwellings
- **Lakes**: Constance, Zurich, Neuchâtel, Bourget
- **Preservation**: Waterlogged organics (textiles, wood, food)
- **Dendrochronology**: Exact annual dates
- **UNESCO**: 111 sites World Heritage

### Mediterranean
- **Mudbrick**: Rare in Europe proper
- **Stone foundations**: Timber superstructure
- **Caves**: Continued use (Sardinia, Balkans)

## Bronze Age (2,300–800 BCE)

### Byre-Houses (Longhouse Evolution)
- **Nordic**: Two/three-aisled, massive oak trenches
- **Urnfield**: Sunken floors, three-aisled
- **British**: Rare; roundhouses dominant

### Regional Variations
- **Denmark/S. Sweden**: Two-aisled, low pitch
- **N. Germany/Netherlands**: Three-aisled, deep trenches
- **Central Europe**: Sunken floors, byre integration

## Iron Age (800–50 BCE)

### Roundhouses (Britain, Atlantic Europe)
- **Post-ring**: 15-25 posts, conical thatch
- **Diameter**: 8-15 m
- **Entrance**: SE-facing, porch
- **Development**: Simple → complex (annexes, ring-grooves)

### Hillforts
- **Ramparts**: Timber-laced, stone-faced
- **Interior**: Roundhouse clusters, granaries
- **Function**: Defense, status, seasonal aggregation

### Oppida (Late Iron Age)
- **Urban scale**: 10-100+ hectares
- **Layout**: Planned streets, zoning
- **Construction**: Timber, stone, earth
