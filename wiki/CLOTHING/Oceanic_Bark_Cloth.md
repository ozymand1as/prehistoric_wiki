# Oceanic Bark Cloth (Tapa/Kapa)

Bark cloth (tapa in Polynesia, kapa in Hawaii, siapo in Samoa, masi in Fiji) is the signature textile of the Pacific Islands, made by beating the inner bark of trees into flexible sheets without weaving. [1]

## Overview

Bark cloth represents a non-woven textile tradition that predates weaving in the Pacific. Made primarily from paper mulberry (Broussonetia papyrifera), it served for clothing, ceremonial use, bedding, and trade across Oceania.

## Production Process

### Tree Cultivation
- **Paper mulberry**: Primary species, cultivated in gardens [2]
- **Harvest**: 2-3 year stems, wet season (bark peels easily)
- **Sustainability**: Coppicing allows repeated harvests

### Processing Steps
1. **Stripping**: Outer bark removed (shell, bamboo knife)
2. **Soaking**: Inner bark (bast) soaked hours to days
3. **Beating**: Wooden beaters (ike, tutua) on wooden anvil [3]
   - Grooved faces for different stages
   - Fibers interlock mechanically
4. **Joining**: Overlapping edges beaten together for large sheets
5. **Drying**: Sun-dried on frames
6. **Decoration**: Painting, stamping, dyeing

## Regional Variations

| Region | Name | Species | Characteristics |
|--------|------|---------|-----------------|
| **Hawaii** | Kapa | Paper mulberry, mamaki | Watermarks, layered colors, perfume |
| **Samoa** | Siapo | Paper mulberry | Brown/black dyes, geometric stamps |
| **Tonga** | Ngatu | Paper mulberry | Large ceremonial pieces, collaborative |
| **Fiji** | Masi | Paper mulberry, fig | Stenciled patterns, ceremonial |
| **Cook Islands** | Tapa | Paper mulberry | Freehand painting |
| **Papua New Guinea** | Tapa | Fig, breadfruit | Body masks, ceremonial |

## Uses

| Category | Items |
|----------|-------|
| **Clothing** | Loincloths, skirts, capes, ponchos, hats |
| **Ceremonial** | Masks, headdresses, wrappings, altar cloths |
| **Domestic** | Bedding, room dividers, mosquito nets |
| **Exchange** | Bridewealth, tribute, peace offerings |
| **Funerary** | Corpse wrapping, grave goods |

## Properties

- **Strength**: High tear resistance (wet), variable dry [4]
- **Flexibility**: Stiffens when new, softens with wear
- **Water resistance**: Moderate; improved with oils/resins
- **Breathability**: Good - porous structure

## Archaeological Evidence

- **Beaters**: Stone/wood in Lapita sites (3,000+ BCE)
- **Impressions**: Bark cloth texture on pottery
- **Contact era**: Historical collections (Cook, Forster)
- **Continuity**: Living traditions = ethnographic analogy
