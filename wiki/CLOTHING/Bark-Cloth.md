# Bark Cloth

Bark cloth is one of the earliest forms of textile, predating woven fabrics, made from the inner bark of trees. It was produced across multiple continents and served as a primary clothing and material source for many indigenous cultures before the widespread introduction of woven textiles.

## History & Development

Bark cloth production dates back to at least the Neolithic period, making it one of the oldest known textile technologies. The practice emerged independently in multiple regions including Africa, Oceania, Southeast Asia, and the Americas. In Africa, bark cloth was a dominant textile for centuries before woven fabrics became available. In Mesoamerica, bark paper (amate) developed as both a material and a writing surface for ritual use. In Oceania, bark cloth (known as tapa) became a widespread and culturally significant textile. The technology persisted in some traditional communities into the modern era.

## Function & Use

- **Primary Use:** Clothing and body covering
- **Secondary Uses:** Writing surface (Mesoamerican amate), ritual and ceremonial objects, wrapping, shelter material, decorative art
- **Cultural Significance:** Bark cloth held deep cultural and spiritual importance in many societies; in Mesoamerica, amate paper was used for ritual codices and ceremonies; in Oceania, tapa was central to gift exchange and status display; in Africa, bark cloth was integral to traditional dress and identity

## Construction & Technique

The process begins by stripping the inner bark from trees such as fig (Ficus), mulberry, and breadfruit. The bark is then soaked in water to soften the fibers. Once softened, the bark is beaten repeatedly—often with wooden mallets—until the fibers separate and fuse into a continuous sheet. Multiple sheets can be layered and beaten together to create larger pieces or thicker material. The resulting cloth is flexible, durable, and suitable for further decoration through painting, stamping, or dyeing.

## Regional Variations

- **Africa:** Bark cloth (kozo, mutuba) was a major textile produced from fig and other tree barks; beaten into sheets and used for clothing and ritual purposes until the introduction of woven fabrics
- **Oceania:** Bark cloth (tapa) was widely produced across Polynesia, Melanesia, and Micronesia; often decorated with geometric patterns through stamping and painting; used for clothing, ceremonial gifts, and wall coverings
- **Mesoamerica:** Bark paper (amate) was made from fig bark and used as a writing surface for codices and ritual documents; production continues in some indigenous communities in Mexico
- **Southeast Asia:** Bark cloth was produced from various tree species and used for clothing and household items
