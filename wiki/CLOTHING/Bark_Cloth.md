# Bark Cloth (Tapa/Kapa)

Bark cloth is a non-woven textile made by beating the inner bark of certain trees into flexible sheets. It represents one of humanity's earliest textile technologies, predating weaving in many regions, and remains culturally significant across the tropics. [1]

## Overview

Bark cloth (tapa in Polynesia, kapa in Hawaii, amate in Mesoamerica, lubugo in Uganda) is produced by harvesting, soaking, and beating the bast (inner bark) of specific trees. The fibers interlock through mechanical beating, creating a fabric without spinning or weaving. It is the primary traditional textile of the Pacific and important in Africa and the Americas.

## Key Species

| Species | Region | Bark Cloth Name | Qualities |
|---------|--------|-----------------|-----------|
| **Paper Mulberry** (Broussonetia papyrifera) | Pacific, SE Asia, introduced globally | Tapa, kapa, siapo | Finest, whitest, strongest |
| **Fig** (Ficus spp.) | Africa, Americas, Asia | Lubugo, amate, various | Coarser, varied colors |
| **Breadfruit** (Artocarpus altilis) | Pacific | 'Ulu tapa | Thicker, durable |
| **Banyan** (Ficus benghalensis) | India, SE Asia | Various | Large sheets possible |
| **Ceiba** (Ceiba pentandra) | Mesoamerica, S. America | Amate (sometimes) | Soft, light-colored |
| **Daphne** (Daphne spp.) | Himalaya, China | Lokta paper | Fine, insect-resistant |
| **Mitsumata** (Edgeworthia chrysantha) | Japan, Korea | Washi paper | High-quality paper/cloth |

## Production Process

### 1. Harvesting
- **Timing**: Wet season (bark peels easily), young branches (2-3 years) [2]
- **Method**: Circular cuts + longitudinal slit, peel in one piece
- **Sustainability**: Coppicing - tree regenerates, repeated harvests

### 2. Preparation
- **Stripping**: Outer bark scraped off (shell, stone, bamboo knife) [3]
- **Inner bark (bast) separated** - Only phloem used
- **Soaking**: Water (hours to days) - softens, begins retting
- **Fermentation** (some traditions): Controlled microbial action loosens fibers

### 3. Beating (Core Process)
- **Tools**: Wooden beaters (ike in Hawaii, tutua in Samoa) [4]
  - Grooved/ribbed faces for different stages
  - Single or double-sided, various sizes
- **Anvil**: Hard wood log, stone, or banana stump
- **Technique**: 
  1. Initial heavy beating - separate fibers, widen sheet
  2. Progressive lighter beating - refine, smooth, felt fibers
  3. Final smoothing - polished beater, sometimes burnishing [5]
- **Joining**: Overlapping edges beaten together for larger sheets

### 4. Finishing
- **Drying**: Sun-dried on frames or flat
- **Decoration**: 
  - Freehand painting (natural dyes)
  - Stamping (carved bamboo/wood stamps - 'ohe kāpala in Hawaii) [6]
  - Rubbing over patterned boards
  - Dyeing (overall or resist)
- **Softening**: Re-beating, rubbing with shell/stone, oiling

## Regional Traditions

### Polynesia (Most Developed)
- **Hawaii (Kapa)**: Highest refinement, watermarks, layered colors, perfume
- **Samoa (Siapo)**: Distinctive geometric patterns, brown/black dyes
- **Tonga (Ngatu)**: Large ceremonial pieces, collaborative production
- **Fiji (Masi)**: Stenciled patterns, ceremonial exchange
- **Cook Islands, Tahiti, Marquesas**: Distinctive regional styles

### Melanesia
- **New Guinea**: Tapa from fig, breadfruit; body masks, ceremonial
- **Vanuatu, New Caledonia**: Less common, replaced by weaving

### Africa
- **Uganda (Buganda - Lubugo)**: Fig bark, royal cloth, UNESCO heritage
- **Congo, Cameroon**: Various fig species, clothing, bedding
- **West Africa**: Baobab, fig; diminishing tradition

### Mesoamerica
- **Aztec (Amate)**: Fig (Ficus), paper for codices, tribute item
- **Maya (Huun)**: Fig, ceremonial, writing material
- **Otomí (Modern)**: Commercial production, tourist art

### South America
- **Amazon**: Various figs, ceremonial, limited clothing
- **Andes**: Less common (weaving dominant), some cloud forest use

## Properties & Uses

| Property | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **Strength** | High tear resistance when wet; variable dry |
| **Flexibility** | Stiff when new, softens with wear/working |
| **Water resistance** | Moderate; oils/resins improve |
| **Breathability** | Good - porous structure |
| **Insulation** | Moderate; layered for warmth |
| **Durability** | Months to years depending on use/care |

### Uses
- **Clothing**: Loincloths, skirts, capes, ponchos, hats
- **Ceremonial**: Masks, headdresses, wrappings, altar cloths
- **Domestic**: Bedding, room dividers, mosquito nets
- **Writing/Painting**: Amate codices, Hawaiian kapa prints
- **Exchange**: Bridewealth, tribute, peace offerings, currency
- **Funerary**: Corpse wrapping, grave goods

## Archaeological Evidence

- **Preservation**: Rare (organic, tropical climates)
- **Beaters**: Stone/wood beaters in Pacific sites (3,000+ BCE)
- **Impressions**: Bark cloth texture on pottery (Lapita, Polynesian)
- **Contact era**: Historical collections (Cook, Forster, missionary)
- **Continuity**: Living traditions = ethnographic analogy
