# Pottery Techniques

---
**Period:** Prehistoric to Present
**Region(s):** Global (Europe, Asia, Africa, Americas, Oceania)
**Culture(s):** Neolithic cultures worldwide
**Time Range:** 29,000 BCE – Present
**Confidence:** High
**Evidence Type:** Material

## Overview
Pottery is the process and products of forming vessels and other objects with clay and other raw materials, which are fired at high temperatures to give them a hard and durable form. Pottery is one of the oldest human inventions, originating before the Neolithic period, with ceramic objects such as the Gravettian Venus of Dolní Věstonice figurine discovered in the Czech Republic dating back to 29,000–25,000 BC. However, the earliest known pottery vessels were discovered in Jiangxi, China, which date back to 18,000 BC. Other early Neolithic and pre-Neolithic pottery artifacts have been found in Jōmon Japan (10,500 BC), the Russian Far East (14,000 BC), Sub-Saharan Africa (9,400 BC), South America (9,000s–7,000s BC), and the Middle East (7,000s–6,000s BC).

## History & Development

### Prehistoric Pottery (Pre-Neolithic)
- **Gravettian period:** Venus of Dolní Věstonice figurine (29,000–25,000 BC) - earliest known ceramic object
- **Jōmon period, Japan:** 10,500 BC
- **Russian Far East:** 14,000 BC
- **Sub-Saharan Africa:** 9,400 BC
- **South America:** 9,000s–7,000s BC
- **Middle East:** 7,000s–6,000s BC

### Neolithic Pottery Development
The earliest forms of pottery were made from clays fired at low temperatures, initially in pit-fires or in open bonfires. They were hand-formed and undecorated. Earthenware can be fired as low as 600°C (1,112°F), and is normally fired below 1,200°C (2,190°F).

**Firing Techniques:**
- **Pit Firing:** Oldest known method, with examples dated between 29,000–25,000 BCE. This technique involves nestling unfired pots in a pit and was used for thousands of years before kilns.
- **Bonfire Firing:** Open bonfires used for early pottery production
- **Kilns:** Earliest known kiln dates to approximately 6000 BCE (Yarim Tepe, modern Iraq). Kilns allow for higher temperatures (exceeding 900°C/1652°F), greater fuel efficiency, and permanent chemical and physical alteration of the clay body.
- **Atmospheric Control:** Experimental reconstructions of Late Neolithic Vinča pottery indicate use of reduced firing in pits, open-air firing, and intentional blackening of vessels fired in oxidizing atmospheres.

### Regional Development in Europe
While it was generally believed that pottery moved from the Northern Balkans to Central Europe, recent findings suggest independent traditions existed and that technology dispersed among European hunter-gatherers faster than previously thought. The Ertebølle culture (5th millennium BCE) in Scandinavia/Germany shows an autochthonous (indigenous) development of ceramic technology.

**Early Experimental Sites:**
- **Çemka Höyük (Türkiye):** Some of the earliest intentional firing and experimental pottery, dating to the Pre-Pottery Neolithic A (c. 9500 BCE). However, this site has been impacted by modern roadbuilding.

### Neolithic Pottery Types
Ceramic vessels have been used for cooking since antiquity due to excellent thermal retention and even heat distribution.

**Three Main Types:**
1. **Earthenware:** Fired at low temperatures (600–1,200°C), porous, limited utility for storing liquids or as tableware. Made from a wide variety of clays, some firing to buff, brown, or black color. Reddish-colored varieties are called terracotta.
2. **Stoneware:** Fired at high temperatures (1,100–1,200°C), stronger and non-porous to liquids. Very tough and practical, much used for kitchen or storage rather than the table. Remained a specialty of Germany until the Renaissance.
3. **Porcelain:** Made by heating materials including kaolin in kilns to temperatures between 1,200 and 1,400°C. Higher temperature than other types, requiring long struggle to achieve. Toughness, strength, and translucence arise mainly from vitrification and formation of the mineral mullite within the body at these high temperatures. First made in China, exported during Tang dynasty (AD 618–906).

### Pottery Production Process
The production of pottery includes the following stages:
1. **Preparing the clay body:** Kneading to ensure even moisture content throughout, removing air (de-airing/pugging), wedging to produce even moisture content
2. **Shaping:** Hand-building, potter's wheel, press moulding, granulate pressing
3. **Drying:** Allowing the formed pottery to dry before firing
4. **Firing:** Heating in pit, bonfire, or kiln
5. **Glazing and decorating:** Can be undertaken before or after firing; additional firing stages after decoration may be needed

### Clay Bodies and Raw Materials
**Main Clay Types:**
- **Kaolin (china clay):** Key ingredient in porcelain, first used in China around the 7th and 8th centuries
- **Ball clay:** Extremely plastic, fine grained sedimentary clay, may contain some organic matter
- **Fire clay:** Slightly lower percentage of fluxes than kaolin, highly heat-resistant, used to increase firing temperature
- **Stoneware clay:** Many characteristics of both fire clay and ball clay, finer grain like ball clay, more heat-resistant like fire clay
- **Common red clay and shale clay:** Vegetable and ferric oxide impurities, useful for bricks but generally unsatisfactory for pottery except under special conditions
- **Bentonite:** Extremely plastic clay added in small quantities to short clay to increase plasticity

**Non-Clay Materials:**
- **Feldspar:** Fluxes which lower vitrification temperature of bodies
- **Quartz:** Attenuates drying shrinkage
- **Nepheline syenite:** Alternative to feldspar
- **Calcined alumina:** Enhances fired properties of a body
- **Chamotte (grog):** Fired clay crushed and sometimes milled, attenuates drying shrinkage
- **Bone ash:** Produced by calcination of animal bone, key raw material for bone china
- **Frit:** Produced by quenching and breaking up glass of specific composition, used in glazes or enamel, or for fritware body
- **Dolomite, limestone, talc, wollastonite:** Various others at low levels of addition

## Function & Use

### Archaeological Significance
Pottery serves as a primary source of archaeological data, often surviving as diagnostic fragments long after artifacts made of organic materials have decayed. When synthesized with broader archaeological evidence, analysis of ceramic assemblages is instrumental in constructing theories regarding a society's socioeconomic organization, economic prosperity, and cultural evolution.

**Fabric Analysis:** Used to analyze the fabric of pottery to understand the archaeological culture of the excavated site by studying:
- Fabric of artifacts, such as their usage, source material composition, decorative pattern, color of patterns
- Characteristics, sophistication, habits, technology, tools, trade
- Usage patterns, source material composition
- Decorative patterns, colors
- Evidence of type of use

**Fabric Analysis Categories:**
1. How pottery was made: material, design, shape and style
2. Decorations: patterns, colors of patterns, slipped (glazing), or unslipped decoration
3. Evidence of type of use

**Trace-Element Analysis:** Mostly by neutron activation, allows sources of clay to be accurately identified. Thermoluminescence test can be used to provide estimate of date of last firing.

**Magnetic Field Recording:** Examining sherds from prehistory, scientists learned that during high-temperature firing, iron materials in clay record the state of the Earth's magnetic field at that moment.

### Regional Applications
**Early Sub-Saharan Africa:** Methods used to produce pottery fall into three categories: techniques visible (decoration, firing, and post-firing techniques), techniques related to the materials (selection or processing of clay, etc.), and techniques for molding or fashioning the clay.

**Patterns of Technical Diffusion:** Patterns of technical diffusion in pot-making visible in archaeological findings also reveal patterns of societal interaction and cross-cultural interaction.

### Regional Variations
**Northern Balkans to Central Europe:** Tradition of pottery development
**Scandinavia/Germany:** Ertebølle culture (5th millennium BCE) - autochthonous development of ceramic technology
**Middle East:** Yarim Tepe (modern Iraq) - earliest known kiln c. 6000 BCE
**Europe:** Independent traditions existed, technology dispersed among European hunter-gatherers faster than previously thought

## Construction & Technique

### Shaping Techniques

**Hand-Building (Earliest Method):**
- Wares constructed by hand from coils of clay
- Combining flat slabs of clay
- Pinching solid balls of clay
- Some combination of these methods
- Parts of hand-built vessels often joined with aid of slip
- Studio potters find hand-building more conducive for one-of-a-kind works of art

**Potter's Wheel:**
- Process called "throwing" (from Old English word *thrawan* meaning to twist or turn)
- Ball of clay placed in center of turntable (wheel-head)
- Potter rotates with stick, foot power, or variable-speed electric motor
- Wheel rotates while solid ball of soft clay is pressed, squeezed, and gently pulled upwards and outwards into hollow shape
- Skill and experience required to throw pots to acceptable standard
- High artistic merit possible, but method's reproducibility is poor
- Can only be used to create wares with radial symmetry on vertical axis

**Press Moulding:**
- Simple technique of shaping by manually pressing lump of clay body into porous mould

**Granulate Pressing (Dust Pressing):**
- Highly automated technique of shaping by pressing clay body in semi-dry and granulated form in mould
- Body pressed into mould by porous die through which water pumped at high pressure
- Fine, free-flowing granulated body prepared by spray drying high-solids content slip
- Widely used in manufacture of ceramic tiles and increasingly plates

### Clay Preparation
Before being shaped, clay must be prepared:
- **Kneading:** Ensure even moisture content throughout the body
- **De-airing/Pugging:** Remove air trapped within clay body (machine called vacuum pug or manually by wedging)
- **Wedging:** Helps produce even moisture content

### Temper and Inclusions
Specific materials added to clay for stability:
- **Grass-tempered pottery:** Found in Slovakia (~5800 BCE)
- **Vegetable inclusions:** Observed in pottery from Makri
- **Chamotte/grog:** Fired clay crushed and milled, attenuates drying shrinkage

## Regional Variations

### Europe
**Northern Balkans:** Tradition of pottery development, Northern Balkans to Central Europe pottery movement
**Scandinavia/Germany:** Ertebølle culture (5th millennium BCE) - autochthonous development of ceramic technology
**Central Europe:** Independent traditions existed, technology dispersed among European hunter-gatherers faster than previously thought

### Asia
**China:** Earliest pottery vessels discovered in Jiangxi (18,000 BC), porcelain first made in China during Tang dynasty (AD 618–906)
**Japan:** Jōmon period pottery (10,500 BC), stoneware specialty
**Middle East:** Yarim Tepe (modern Iraq) - earliest known kiln c. 6000 BCE

### Sub-Saharan Africa
**Methods Categories:**
1. Techniques visible (decoration, firing, post-firing techniques) - readily imitated, indicate distant connection between groups, trade in same market, or relatively close settlements
2. Techniques related to materials (selection or processing of clay) - require more studied replication, indicate closer connection between people
3. Techniques for molding or fashioning the clay - require ability to communicate effectively, pre-existing norms of contact, or shared language

## Uncertainties & Research Questions
- Firing procedures for certain cultures, specifically the Late Neolithic Vinča, remain largely unknown and are currently being reconstructed through experimental archaeology
- Some early sites (e.g., Çemka Höyük) have been impacted by modern roadbuilding, which may affect availability of archaeological data
- Exact timing of independent versus transmitted pottery traditions in different regions remains uncertain
- How early experimental pottery was used and its functional significance is unclear
