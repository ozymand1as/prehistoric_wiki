# Neolithic Pottery

Pottery is one of the oldest human inventions, originating before the Neolithic period. Ceramic objects such as the Gravettian Venus of Dolní Věstonice figurine (29,000–25,000 BC) predate pottery vessels, with the earliest known vessels discovered in Jiangxi, China (18,000 BC). ## Overview

Pottery is one of the oldest human inventions, originating before the Neolithic period. Ceramic objects such as the Gravettian Venus of Dolní Věstonice figurine (29,000–25,000 BC) predate pottery vessels, with the earliest known vessels discovered in Jiangxi, China (18,000 BC). The invention of pottery marked the transition from hunter-gatherer to settled agricultural societies, requiring usable clay, fire hot enough to fire pottery, a sedentary lifestyle, and sufficient economic need.
## History & Development

### Pre-Neolithic Origins

- **29,000–25,000 BC:** Earliest ceramic objects — Gravettian Venus of Dolní Věstonice figurine (Czech Republic) [2]
- **18,000 BC:** Earliest pottery vessels — Jiangxi, China
- **14,000 BC:** Russian Far East
- **10,500 BC:** Jōmon Japan (cord-marked pottery)
- **9,400 BC:** Sub-Saharan Africa (Ounjougou, Mali)
- **9,000s–7,000s BC:** South America
- **7,000s–6,000s BC:** Middle East

### Neolithic Pottery Production Characteristics

- Firing at low temperatures (600–1,200°C) in pit-fires or open bonfires [3]
- Hand-formed and undecorated
- Earthenware: porous, limited liquid storage utility
- Clay tempered with sand, grit, crushed shell, or crushed pottery to prevent cracking
- Rounded bottoms to avoid sharp angles susceptible to cracking
- No glazing on earliest pots
- Weights for looms or fishing nets were common early uses [4]

### Firing Methods

- **Pit Firing:** Oldest method, examples dated 29,000–25,000 BCE
- **Bonfire Firing:** Open fires used for early pottery production [5]
- **Kiln Firing:** Earliest known kiln c. 6000 BCE at Yarim Tepe, modern Iraq
- **Transition:** From pit-kilns (holes dug in ground, covered with fuel) to more sophisticated kilns

### Pottery Neolithic Period (Fertile Crescent)

- **Hassuna period (7000–6500 BC):** Hand-formed, undecorated, low-fired pottery [6]
- **Halaf period (6500–5500 BC):** Elaborate painted designs, high technical competence
- **Ubaid period (5500–4000 BC):** Potter's wheel invented, kiln technology improved
- **Uruk period (4000–3100 BC):** Widespread pottery production

### Key Innovation Timeline

- 29,000–25,000 BC: Earliest ceramic objects (figurines, not vessels) [7]
- 18,000 BC: Earliest pottery vessels (China)
- 6000 BC: Earliest known kiln (Yarim Tepe, Iraq)
- 5th millennium BC: Potter's wheel invented (Europe, Cucuteni-Trypillia culture)
- 18th century: Porcelain production outside East Asia begins (Meissen, Germany, 1709)

## Function & Use

- **Primary Use:** Food storage, cooking, brewing
- **Secondary Uses:** Social signaling, ritual objects, trade goods, weights for looms or fishing nets
- **Cultural Significance:** First technologies requiring resource management and long-term planning; marked transition from hunter-gatherer to settled agricultural societies

## Construction & Technique

- **Materials:** Local clay, temper (sand, grit, crushed shell, crushed pottery), pigments
- **Techniques:** Hand-building (coiling, slab-building, pinching, slip joining), potter's wheel (throwing), press molding, slip casting
- **Firing:** Pit firing, bonfire firing, kiln firing
- **Clay Preparation:** Kneading for even moisture, de-airing (pugging) or wedging to remove trapped air
- **Clay Body Composition:** Clay matrix (<0.02mm grains) + clay inclusions (larger grains)
- **Tools Required:** Clay, water, firing materials, moulds (for press molding, slip casting)

## Regional Variations

- **Europe:** Oldest pottery circa 6700 BC (Samara River, Russia, Yelshanka culture); Linear Pottery culture 5500–4500 BC; Minoan pottery with complex painted decoration and natural themes; Greek pottery with red-figure and black-figure techniques; Etruscan pottery heavily influenced by Greek pottery; Roman pottery with moulded decoration and industrial-scale production (Samian ware); Medieval coarse utilitarian wares; European porcelain not made effectively until 18th century (Meissen, Germany, 1709)

- **Near East / Fertile Crescent:** Divided into Hassuna (7000–6500 BC), Halaf (6500–5500 BC), Ubaid (5500–4000 BC), Uruk (4000–3100 BC); earliest forms hand-formed from slabs, undecorated, unglazed, low-fired reddish-brown clays; potter's wheel invented in Mesopotamia (6000–4000 BC, Ubaid period); kilns could fire to 1,050–1,200°C; glazing commonly used, pottery more decorated; Halafian pottery achieved high technical competence

- **Americas:** Independent development of pottery; earliest dates Brazil (9,500–5,000 years ago, 7,000–6,000 years ago); Mesoamerica Archaic Era (3500–2000 BC), Formative period (2000 BC–AD 200); Maya ceramics with finely painted vessels and elaborate scenes; Olmec terracotta sculpture; Moche portrait vessels; no stoneware, porcelain, or glazes developed (unlike Old World)

- **Africa:** Oldest pottery outside East Asia at Ounjougou, Mali (9,400 BC); Bosumpra Cave, Ghana with well-manufactured pottery from early 10th millennium BC; ceramics arrived with Bantu language spread (controversial); three production method categories: visible techniques, material techniques, molding techniques

- **East Asia:** Jōmon period Japan (10,500 BC) cord-marked pottery; Chinese stoneware as early as 15th century BC; Chinese porcelain Tang dynasty (AD 618–906) perfected by Song dynasty; Korean potters adopted porcelain by 14th century AD

## Uncertainties & Research Questions

- Firing procedures for certain cultures, specifically the Late Neolithic Vinča, remain largely unknown and are currently being reconstructed through experimental archaeology
- Some early sites (e.g., Çemka Höyük) have been impacted by modern roadbuilding, which may affect availability of archaeological data
- Exact timing of independent versus transmitted pottery traditions in different regions remains uncertain
- How early experimental pottery was used and its functional significance is unclear
- The controversy surrounding the arrival of ceramics with Bantu language spread in Sub-Saharan Africa requires further research
