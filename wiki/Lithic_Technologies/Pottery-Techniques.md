# Lithic Technologies/Pottery Techniques

---
**Period:** Prehistoric to Present
**Region(s):** Global (Europe, Asia, Africa, Americas, Oceania)
**Culture(s):** Neolithic cultures worldwide, Cucuteni-Trypillia, Minoan, Greek, Etruscan, Roman, Maya, Olmec, Moche, Jōmon
**Time Range:** 29,000 BCE – Present
**Confidence:** High
**Evidence Type:** Material

## Overview
Pottery is the process and products of forming vessels and other objects with clay and other raw materials, which are fired at high temperatures to give them a hard and durable form. Pottery is one of the oldest human inventions, originating before the Neolithic period, with ceramic objects such as the Gravettian Venus of Dolní Věstonice figurine discovered in the Czech Republic dating back to 29,000–25,000 BC. The earliest known pottery vessels were discovered in Jiangxi, China, dating back to 18,000 BC. Pottery techniques range from hand-building (the earliest method) to the potter's wheel, press moulding, slip casting, and modern 3D printing.

## History & Development

### Prehistoric Pottery (Pre-Neolithic)
- **Gravettian period:** Venus of Dolní Věstonice figurine (29,000–25,000 BC) — earliest known ceramic object
- **Jōmon period, Japan:** 10,500 BC
- **Russian Far East:** 14,000 BC
- **Sub-Saharan Africa:** 9,400 BC
- **South America:** 9,000s–7,000s BC
- **Middle East:** 7,000s–6,000s BC

### Neolithic Pottery Development
The earliest forms of pottery were made from clays fired at low temperatures, initially in pit-fires or in open bonfires. They were hand-formed and undecorated. Earthenware can be fired as low as 600°C (1,112°F), and is normally fired below 1,200°C (2,192°F).

**Firing Methods:**
- **Pit Firing:** Oldest known method, with examples dated between 29,000–25,000 BCE. This technique involves nestling unfired pots in a pit and was used for thousands of years before kilns.
- **Bonfire Firing:** Open bonfires used for early pottery production.
- **Kiln Firing:** Earliest known kiln dates to approximately 6000 BCE (Yarim Tepe, modern Iraq). Kilns allow for higher temperatures (exceeding 900°C/1652°F), greater fuel efficiency, and permanent chemical and physical alteration of the clay body.
- **Atmospheric Control:** Experimental reconstructions of Late Neolithic Vinča pottery indicate use of reduced firing in pits, open-air firing, and intentional blackening of vessels fired in oxidizing atmospheres.

### Pottery Neolithic Period (Fertile Crescent)
- **Hassuna period (7000–6500 BC):** Hand-formed, undecorated, low-fired pottery
- **Halaf period (6500–5500 BC):** Elaborate painted designs, high technical competence
- **Ubaid period (5500–4000 BC):** Potter's wheel invented, kiln technology improved
- **Uruk period (4000–3100 BC):** Widespread pottery production

### Regional Development in Europe
While it was generally believed that pottery moved from the Northern Balkans to Central Europe, recent findings suggest independent traditions existed and that technology dispersed among European hunter-gatherers faster than previously thought. The Ertebølle culture (5th millennium BCE) in Scandinavia/Germany shows an autochthonous (indigenous) development of ceramic technology.

**Early Experimental Sites:**
- **Çemka Höyük (Türkiye):** Some of the earliest intentional firing and experimental pottery, dating to the Pre-Pottery Neolithic A (c. 9500 BCE). However, this site has been impacted by modern roadbuilding.

### Key Innovation Timeline
- 29,000–25,000 BC: Earliest ceramic objects (figurines, not vessels)
- 18,000 BC: Earliest pottery vessels (China)
- 6000 BC: Earliest known kiln (Yarim Tepe, Iraq)
- 5th millennium BC: Potter's wheel invented (Europe, Cucuteni-Trypillia culture, western Ukraine)
- 18th century: Porcelain production outside East Asia begins (Meissen, Germany, 1709)

## Function & Use
- **Primary Use:** Food storage, cooking, brewing
- **Secondary Uses:** Social signaling, ritual objects, trade goods, weights for looms or fishing nets
- **Cultural Significance:** First technologies requiring resource management and long-term planning; marked transition from hunter-gatherer to settled agricultural societies

## Construction & Technique

### Clay Preparation
Before being shaped, clay must be prepared:
- **Kneading:** Ensure even moisture content throughout the body
- **De-airing/Pugging:** Remove air trapped within clay body (machine called vacuum pug or manually by wedging)
- **Wedging:** Helps produce even moisture content
- **Clay Body Composition:** Clay matrix (<0.02mm grains) + clay inclusions (larger grains)

### Pottery Making Techniques

**Hand-Building (Earliest Method):**
- Coiling: Clay shaped from coils of clay, joined with slip
- Slab-building: Combining flat slabs of clay
- Pinching: Shaping solid balls of clay
- Parts joined with aid of slip (clay + water mixture)

**Potter's Wheel (Throwing):**
- Invented in Europe in the 5th millennium BC; earliest potter's wheel from Cucuteni-Trypillia culture (western Ukraine), mid-5th millennium BC
- Process called "throwing" (from Old English *thrawan* = to twist/turn)
- Ball of clay placed on wheel-head, rotated by stick, foot power, or electric motor
- Clay pressed, squeezed, and pulled upwards into hollow shape
- Limited to radial symmetry on vertical axis
- Skill-intensive; poor reproducibility

**Other Techniques:**
- **Press Moulding:** Pressing clay into porous mould
- **Jiggering/Jolleying:** Wheel operations for flatware and hollowware, used since 18th century
- **Slip Casting:** Liquid clay poured into absorbent plaster mould; suited for complex shapes
- **RAM Pressing:** Clay pressed between two porous moulding plates
- **Granulate Pressing (Dust Pressing):** Semi-dry granulated clay pressed with high-pressure water
- **3D Printing:** Layered deposition of soft clay or powder binding

### Stages of Production
1. Preparing clay body (kneading, de-airing, wedging)
2. Shaping (hand-building, wheel, moulding, etc.)
3. Drying (greenware → plastic → leather-hard → bone-dry)
4. Firing (biscuit/bisque firing)
5. Glazing and decorating (glaze firing)

### Associated Technologies

**Kilns:**
- **Pit-kilns/Trench-kilns:** Holes dug in ground, covered with fuel; provided insulation and better firing control; earliest intentionally constructed kilns
- **Bottle kiln:** Common historical design
- **Tunnel kiln:** Modern design
- **Monitoring:** Pyrometers, thermocouples, pyrometric devices
- **Firing Temperatures:** Earthenware 600–1,200°C; Stoneware 1,100–1,300°C; Porcelain 1,200–1,400°C

**Fuel Sources:**
- Wood (traditional, introduces smoke/soot/ash)
- Coal (traditional, affects appearance of unprotected wares)
- Gas (cleaner, more controllable)
- Electricity (clean, precise control)
- Animal dung (traditional, sustainable)
- Rice chaff, dried seaweed, olive stones, nut shells (sustainable traditional fuels)
- Waste Vegetable Oil / Biodiesel (modern, cleaner alternatives)
- Biomass pellets (automated, consistent)
- Biogas (replaces fossil-based methane)
- Hydrogen (milestone reached early 2025 with large-scale pilot kilns, 100% hydrogen)
- Microwave energy (investigated experimentally)
- **Saggars:** Ceramic boxes to protect wares from fuel contamination

**Firing Atmosphere:**
- **Oxidizing:** Oxygen present; iron oxides produce brown-red colors
- **Reducing:** Oxygen-deficient; produces darker colors including black (FeO)
- Controlled by restricting air supply or supplying excess fuel

## Regional Variations

### Europe
- **Northern Balkans:** Tradition of pottery development, Northern Balkans to Central Europe pottery movement
- **Scandinavia/Germany:** Ertebølle culture (5th millennium BCE) — autochthonous development of ceramic technology
- **Central Europe:** Independent traditions existed, technology dispersed among European hunter-gatherers faster than previously thought
- **Greece:** Red-figure and black-figure techniques, wheel in regular use
- **Rome:** Moulded decoration, industrial-scale production (Samian ware)
- **Medieval Europe:** Coarse utilitarian wares; elites ate off metal vessels
- **Meissen, Germany (1709):** First effective European porcelain

### Asia
- **China:** Earliest pottery vessels discovered in Jiangxi (18,000 BC); stoneware as early as 15th century BC; porcelain Tang dynasty (AD 618–906) perfected by Song dynasty
- **Japan:** Jōmon period pottery (10,500 BC), cord-marked pottery
- **Korea:** Potters adopted porcelain by 14th century AD
- **Middle East:** Yarim Tepe (modern Iraq) — earliest known kiln c. 6000 BCE

### Sub-Saharan Africa
- **Oldest pottery outside East Asia:** Ounjougou, Mali (9,400 BC)
- **Bosumpra Cave, Ghana:** Well-manufactured pottery from early 10th millennium BC
- **Bantu spread:** Ceramics arrived with Bantu language spread (controversial)
- **Three production method categories:**
  1. Visible techniques (decoration, firing, post-firing techniques)
  2. Material techniques (selection or processing of clay)
  3. Molding techniques (fashioning the clay)

### Americas
- **Independent development of pottery**
- **Earliest dates:** Brazil (9,500–5,000 years ago, 7,000–6,000 years ago)
- **Mesoamerica:** Archaic Era (3500–2000 BC), Formative period (2000 BC–AD 200)
- **Maya:** Finely painted vessels with elaborate scenes
- **Olmec:** Terracotta sculpture
- **Moche:** Portrait vessels
- **No stoneware, porcelain, or glazes developed** (unlike Old World)

## Uncertainties & Research Questions
- Firing procedures for certain cultures, specifically the Late Neolithic Vinča, remain largely unknown and are currently being reconstructed through experimental archaeology
- Some early sites (e.g., Çemka Höyük) have been impacted by modern roadbuilding, which may affect availability of archaeological data
- Exact timing of independent versus transmitted pottery traditions in different regions remains uncertain
- How early experimental pottery was used and its functional significance is unclear
- The controversy surrounding the arrival of ceramics with Bantu language spread in Sub-Saharan Africa requires further research
- Reproducibility of potter's wheel techniques in Neolithic contexts remains uncertain
