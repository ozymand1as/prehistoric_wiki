# Agriculture and Social Complexity

The adoption of agriculture is the most consequential technological shift in human history. It triggered a cascade of demographic, economic, and social changes that culminated in the emergence of cities, states, and civilization. [1]

## Overview

Agriculture did not cause complexity directly but created the conditions for it: surplus, sedentism, population growth, storage, and defensible resources. The relationship is reciprocal—social complexity also drives agricultural intensification.

## Causal Chain

### 1. Domestication → Surplus
- **Caloric density**: Grains > wild plants
- **Storability**: Seeds last years
- **Predictability**: Scheduled harvests
- **Surplus**: Enables non-producers

### 2. Surplus → Sedentism
- **Storage**: Requires fixed location
- **Investment**: Fields, irrigation, terracing
- **Territoriality**: Defense of investment

### 3. Sedentism → Population Growth
- **Shorter birth intervals**: No carrying infants
- **Earlier weaning**: Gruel, porridge
- **Carrying capacity**: 10-100x forager density

### 4. Population → Social Complexity
- **Scalar stress**: Face-to-face insufficient
- **Specialization**: Craft, trade, ritual, administration [2]
- **Hierarchy**: Coordination, conflict resolution
- **Institutions**: Formal roles, rules, enforcement

## Feedback Loops

### Intensification
- **Population pressure** → **Intensification** (irrigation, terracing, multi-cropping) [3]
- **Intensification** → **Surplus** → **Population growth**

### Elite Control
- **Surplus** → **Elite appropriation** (tax, tribute)
- **Elite** → **Public works** (irrigation, defense) [4]
- **Public works** → **More surplus**

### Warfare
- **Stored surplus** → **Raiding target**
- **Defense** → **Fortifications, warriors**
- **Warriors** → **Elite power, territorial expansion**

## Trajectories by Region

| Region | Founder Crops | First Villages | First Cities | First States |
|--------|---------------|----------------|--------------|--------------|
| **Near East** | Wheat, barley, legumes | 9,500 BCE (Jericho) | 4,000 BCE (Uruk) | 3,000 BCE (Sumer) |

| **Mesoamerica** | Maize, beans, squash | 3,000 BCE (Gulf Coast) | 500 BCE (Monte Albán) | 200 CE (Teotihuacan) |

| **Africa** | Sorghum, millet, yam | 2,000 BCE (Sahel) | 500 BCE (Jenne-jeno) | 800 CE (Ghana) |
| **Eastern NA** | Sunflower, goosefoot | 2,000 BCE (Late Archaic) | 1,000 CE (Cahokia) | None (pre-contact) |

## Theoretical Perspectives

### Managerial (Service, Wittfogel)
- **Irrigation** → **Centralized management** → **State**
- **Coordination problem** requires hierarchy

### Conflict (Carneiro, Haas)
- **Population pressure** + **Circumscription** → **Warfare** → **State**
- **Conquest** creates hierarchy

### Evolutionary (Flannery, Marcus)
- **Dual-processual**: Network (trade) + Corporate (ritual) strategies
- **Agency**: Elites manipulate ideology, economy

### Agency (Pauketat, Cobb)
- **Historical contingency**: Not inevitable
- **Local agency**: Resistance, innovation, fission
