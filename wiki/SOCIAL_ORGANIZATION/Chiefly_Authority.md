# Chiefly Authority

Chiefly authority is the legitimate, institutionalized power of a chief in a ranked society. Unlike big men, chiefs hold hereditary office, command tribute, and exercise coercive power backed by ideology and ritual.

## Overview

Chiefly authority marks the transition from achieved to ascribed leadership. It combines economic control (redistribution), ritual power (sacred status), and coercive capacity (warriors, enforcement).

## Sources of Chiefly Power

### Economic
- **Tribute collection**: Staple/wealth finance
- **Redistribution**: Feasts, rations, public works
- **Trade control**: Monopoly on exotic goods
- **Land allocation**: Authority over territory

### Ritual/Religious
- **Sacred lineage**: Descent from gods/ancestors
- **Mediation**: Intermediary with supernatural
- **Calendar control**: Agricultural/ritual timing
- **Sacrifice**: Authority to perform rites

### Coercive
- **Warrior retinue**: Personal guard, enforcers
- **Fortifications**: Defensive/offensive capacity
- **Judicial**: Arbitration, punishment
- **Labor mobilization**: Corvée for projects

### Ideological
- **Divine right**: Sacred king, god-king
- **Ancestor cult**: Legitimacy through lineage
- **Monumental architecture**: Visible power
- **Iconography**: Royal symbols, regalia

## Chiefly Rank Systems

### Simple Chiefdom (Two-Tier)
- **Chief**: Single paramount
- **Commoners**: Undifferentiated
- **Example**: Early European Bronze Age

### Complex Chiefdom (Three+ Tiers)
- **Paramount chief**: Supreme authority
- **District chiefs**: Regional administration
- **Village heads: Local implementation
- **Example**: Hawaiian Islands, Mississippian

## Succession

### Hereditary Rules
- **Primogeniture**: Eldest son
- **Ultimo geniture**: Youngest son
- **Matrilineal**: Sister's son
- **Election**: Council of nobles

### Succession Crises
- **No heir**: Adoption, election
- **Multiple heirs**: Civil war, fission
- **Regency**: Minor heir, regent
