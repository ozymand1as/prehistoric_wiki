# Metallurgy and Elite Power

The control of metallurgy—from ore procurement to finished objects—was a primary engine of elite power in the Chalcolithic and Bronze Age. Metal's rarity, transformative technology, and utility for weapons and prestige goods made it a strategic resource.

## Overview

Metallurgy is not merely a craft but a political technology. Control over the chaîne opératoire—mining, smelting, casting, finishing—enabled elites to monopolize violence (weapons), display status (prestige goods), and legitimize rule (ritual objects).

## The Metallurgical Chain

### Stages
| Stage | Activity | Control Point |
|-------|----------|---------------|
| **Prospecting** | Ore location | Knowledge secrecy |
| **Mining** | Extraction | Labor control, territory |
| **Smelting** | Ore → metal | Technical secrecy, fuel |
| **Casting** | Metal → form | Moulds, templates |
| **Finishing** | Hammering, annealing | Skill, workshop |
| **Distribution** | Allocation | Elite monopoly |

## Elite Control Mechanisms

### Source Control
- **Direct**: Royal mines (Sinai turquoise, Laurion silver, Nubian gold)
- **Tribute**: Ore/metal from subject regions
- **Trade monopoly**: State merchants (Assyrian karum, Minoan fleet)

### Production Control
- **Attached specialists**: Palace smiths (Mycenaean, Ur III, Shang)
- **Workshop location**: Within palace/citadel
- **Standardization**: Weights, forms, quality marks

### Distribution Control
- **Weapons**: Elite retinue, state army
- **Prestige goods**: Diplomatic gifts, elite burials
- **Ritual objects**: Temple deposits, royal regalia

## Case Studies

### Near East (Chalcolithic–Bronze Age)
- **Uruk**: Temple control of copper, early bronze
- **Ur III**: State bronze industry, rationed smiths
- **Assyrian trade colonies**: Private merchants, state tax
- **Hittite**: Royal monopoly on iron (late)

### Egypt
- **Old Kingdom**: State expeditions (Sinai, Nubia)
- **Middle Kingdom**: Fortresses in Nubia (gold)
- **New Kingdom**: Imperial tribute (gold, copper)

### Aegean (Minoan/Mycenaean)
- **Minoan**: Palace workshops, Linear B records
- **Mycenaean**: Palace smiths, bronze weapons export
- **Linear B**: Smith names, rations, quotas

### Central Asia / Steppe
- **Sintashta/Andronovo**: Chariot, bronze, horse complex
- **BMAC**: Tin sources, lapis, trade
- **Scythian**: Gold, iron, horse gear

### China (Erlitou → Shang → Zhou)
- **Erlitou**: Palace bronze foundries, ritual vessels
- **Shang**: Royal foundries, piece-mould casting
- **Zhou**: Feudal bronze production, inscriptions

### Andes (Moche → Inka)
- **Moche**: Royal workshops, gold/silver
- **Inka**: State bronze, gold/silver monopoly
- **Metallurgy**: Arsenical bronze, tumbaga (Au-Cu)

## Metal as Power

### Weapons
- **Monopoly on violence**: Bronze swords, daggers, armor
- **Chariotry**: Elite mobile strike force
- **Standardization**: State armories

### Prestige Goods
- **Vessels**: Ritual feasting, elite display
- **Ornaments**: Gold, silver, electrum jewelry
- **Ritual objects**: Bells, mirrors, masks, standards

### Currency/Accounting
- **Metal ingots**: Oxhide copper, standardized
- **Silver**: Shekel, debeen, qian standards
- **Hoards**: Elite wealth storage

## Social Implications

### Specialist Status
- **Smiths**: Liminal, ritually powerful, sometimes restricted
- **Miners**: Often coerced, enslaved, or corvée
- **Traders**: State agents or independent

### Gender
- **Mining/smelting**: Male (ritual danger)
- **Finishing/polishing**: Sometimes female
- **Weaving/finishing textiles**: Female
