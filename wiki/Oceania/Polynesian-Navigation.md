# Polynesian Navigation

Polynesian navigation (Polynesian wayfinding) was used for thousands of years to enable long voyages across thousands of kilometres of the open Pacific Ocean. Polynesians made contact with nearly every island within the vast Polynesian Triangle, using outrigger canoes or double-hulled canoes. Navigators used wayfinding techniques such as navigation by the stars, observations of birds, ocean swells, and wind patterns, relying on oral tradition. This island-hopping was a solution to scarcity of useful resources in the Pacific.

## History & Development

### Migration Patterns

Between about 3000 and 1000 BCE, speakers of Austronesian languages spread through Southeast Asia, starting from Taiwan.

**Lapita Culture:** In the mid-2nd millennium BCE, a distinctive culture appeared in north-west Melanesia (Bismarck Archipelago). Characteristic is the making of pottery with varied shapes and patterns.

**Lapita Expansion:** Between 1300 and 900 BCE, the Lapita culture spread 6,000 km to Tonga and Samoa. Lapita pottery eventually died out due to clay scarcity.

**Polynesian Triangle:** Navigation pathways resemble an octopus with head on Ra'iātea (French Polynesia) and tentacles across the Pacific.

### Settlement Chronology

- **Initial settlement:** Cook Islands before 1000 AD
- **Eastern Polynesia:** Society Islands and Marquesas Islands settled first
- **Remote regions:** Hawaii, Easter Island, New Zealand settled later
- **Tuvaluan atolls:** Pattern extended north of Samoa; Tutuvalu provided stepping stone to Polynesian Outlier communities
- **Easter Island:** Natives originated from Mangareva
- **Subantarctic:** Evidence of visits by 1500

### Cosmology and Navigation Knowledge

- Navigation pathways resemble octopus: head centered on Ra'iātea, tentacles spread
- Each island maintained guild of navigators with high status
- Tupaia (navigator on Cook's first voyage) drew chart of 130 islands within 3,200 km

## Function & Use

- **Primary Use:** Long-distance oceanic voyaging and island-hopping across the Pacific
- **Secondary Uses:** Trade between islands, migration and settlement, resource acquisition, cultural exchange
- **Cultural Significance:** Navigators held high status; wayfinding knowledge integral to Polynesian culture survival and expansion

## Construction & Technique

### Canoe Construction

- **Outrigger canoes:** Single hull with lateral support float
- **Double-hulled canoes (wa'a kaulua):** Two large hulls, equal length, lashed side by side
- **Waka:** New Zealand Māori vessels (double-hulled, war, river)
- **Construction methods:** Kept as guild secrets; lashed-lug and sewn-plank techniques

### Navigation Techniques

Polynesian navigation relies on constant observation and memorization.

**Primary Navigation Methods:**
- **Sun:** Main guide; following exact points as it rose and set
- **Stars:** Rising and setting points after sunset
- **Winds and Swells:** Used when no stars visible
- **Birds:** Flight patterns of seabirds
- **Bioluminescence:** Indicated direction to islands
- **Weather:** Wind patterns and observations

**Wayfinding Without Instruments:**
- Navigators detected changes in canoe speed, heading, and time of day
- Knowledge passed orally, often in song form, from master to apprentice

### Star Compass

- **Star compass:** System specifying bearings of selected stars
- Mau Piailug's star compass taught in Caroline Islands
- For equatorial navigators, whole celestial sphere exposed

### Navigation by Ocean Swells

- Swells move in straight direction; easier to determine heading
- Navigators learned effect various islands have on swell shape
- "Canoe-days" used to measure sailing time between islands

### Clouds and Sky Observations

- Clouds from coral atolls reflect heat into sky
- Te lapa: Burst of light on water surface indicating islands
- Subtle color differences in sky indicated lagoons vs. deep water

### Bird Navigation

- Shore-sighting birds: White tern and noddy tern fly out morning, return night
- Frigatebird theory: Feathers drenched if they land; navigators released them near land
- Voyage routes followed bird migrations

## Regional Variations

### Western Polynesia

- Lapita pottery persisted in Samoa, Tonga, Fiji
- Eventually died out due to clay scarcity

### Central Polynesia

- Some ceramic materials retrieved through excavations attributed to trade
- Stick charts used onshore (Marshall Islands)

### Eastern Polynesia

- Navigators sailing from Tahiti to Tuamotus sailed east towards Anaa atoll
- Used reflection of lagoon in clouds

### South Polynesia

- Kermadec Islands, Chatham Islands, Auckland Islands, Norfolk Island
- Chatham Island waka dated to 1440–1470
- Enderby Island settlement dated to 13th Century

### Easter Island (Rapa Nui)

- Natives likely originated from Mangareva
- Discovered using flight path of sooty tern
- No evidence of navigation found by first European visitor

### Subantarctic Islands

- "Ui-te-Rangiora" leading fleet around year 650
- Reached "a place of bitter cold"

## Uncertainties & Research Questions

- Specific chronology of discovery and settlement is debated
- Furthest southern extent of expansion is debated
- Descriptions of early pottery on Antipodes Islands unsubstantiated
- Degree of Pre-Columbian contact between Polynesia and Americas remains debated
