## Overview
Mesoamerican technological traditions encompass the innovations of the major civilizations of central and southern Mexico and northern Central America, spanning over three millennia. These cultures developed sophisticated stone tool technologies, metallurgy, ceramics, agricultural systems, and monumental architecture, with obsidian serving as the dominant lithic material and a trade network of extraordinary scale.

## History & Development
- **Olmec (1500–400 BCE):** Earliest complex civilization of Mesoamerica; Gulf Coast of Mexico; pioneered stone carving (colossal heads), rubber production, and long-distance trade in obsidian, jade, cacao, and ceramics.
- **Maya (2600 BCE–1697 AD):** Developed the most sophisticated writing system (logosyllabic script), advanced astronomy, the Long Count calendar, and monumental architecture including stepped pyramids and corbelled arches.
- **Teotihuacan (200 BCE–800 AD):** Major urban center; talud-tablero architectural style; obsidian workshop monopoly; tripod slab-footed ceramics; Pyramid of the Sun (216 ft tall).
- **Zapotec (500 BC–1500 AD):** Monte Albán; early hieroglyphic writing; developed among the first defensive palisades and adobe structures.
- **Toltec (900–1100 AD):** Central Mexico; influenced Chichén Itzá architecture.
- **Aztec (1325–1521 AD):** Tenochtitlan (largest Mesoamerican city, ~125,000 people); Templo Mayor (100×80 m base, 60 m tall); chinampas (floating gardens); tribute empire.
- **Mixtec:** Dominant goldsmiths of post-Classic Mesoamerica.
- **Purépecha/Tarascan:** Michoacán; actively resisted Aztec domination.

## Function & Use
- **Primary Use:** Stone tool production, monumental construction, agriculture, ritual practice, long-distance trade
- **Secondary Uses:** Personal adornment, warfare, writing and record-keeping, water management, textile production
- **Cultural Significance:** Technologies were deeply embedded in ritual and religious life; obsidian tools held symbolic value; metals associated with solar/lunar deities; ballgame equipment tied to cosmological beliefs; metallurgy and ceramics served elite and ceremonial functions

## Construction & Technique
- **Lithic reduction:** Bifacial, unifacial, and expedient flake tools via lithic reduction; prismatic blade production using pressure flaking from polyhedral cores
- **Stone working:** Pecking, grinding, and carving for figurines and jewelry; limestone worked with stone tools while quarried; sandstone ball court stones weighing 10+ tons
- **Metallurgy:** Lost-wax casting (bells, ornaments), cold-working (ornaments, hand tools), alloying for color properties (vivid gold tones from tin, silver shades from arsenic)
- **Ceramics:** Tripod slab-footed vessels; cantera stone veneer over tezontle; ballgame balls made of solid natural rubber from Castilla elastica mixed with Ipomoea alba sap
- **Agriculture:** Milpa cycle (slash-and-burn), chinampas (floating gardens), Pet kot (Maya forest gardens), irrigation, terracing
- **Nixtamalization:** Maize softened with ashes or lime for processing

## Regional Variations
- **Gulf Coast (Olmec):** Rubber production, colossal stone heads, early obsidian trade networks, cacao cultivation
- **Oaxaca Valley (Zapotec, Maya):** Early pottery at San José Mogote; Monte Albán hieroglyphic writing; Guilá Naquitz cave earliest maize cobs (~4300 BCE) and squash (~8000 BCE)
- **Central Mexico (Teotihuacan, Aztec):** Obsidian workshop monopoly (Teotihuacan: 100+ workshops); talud-tablero architecture; chinampas; Templo Mayor
- **Yucatán (Maya):** Stepped pyramids, corbelled arches, Long Count calendar, logosyllabic script, Pet kot forest gardens
- **West Mexico (Mixtec, Purépecha):** Earliest metallurgy (~800 CE); Mixtec goldsmithing; Purépecha resistance to Aztec expansion
- **Michoacán (Purépecha):** Copper-tin bronze production hub in Phase 2 metallurgy

## Uncertainties & Research Questions
- Exact diffusion route of metallurgy into West Mexico (maritime vs. overland from Central/South America)
- Degree of Aztec distinctiveness in metallurgy vs. inherited traditions
- Precision of dating for earliest metallurgical finds in West Mexico (600 vs. 800 CE)
- Extent of Toltec technological influence on Chichén Itzá vs. independent development
- How the wheel was known but never became technologically relevant in the absence of draft animals
