# Traditional Medicine / Research Findings (Full)

Traditional medicine encompasses the knowledge, practices, and treatments developed by various cultures throughout history, from prehistoric herbal remedies to the formalized medical systems of ancient civilizations. Research findings span paleopharmacological evidence of medicinal plant use dating to ~60,000 years ago, through the first written records of herbal prescriptions by the Sumerians (c. 3000 BCE), to the comprehensive pharmacological texts of Greece and Rome. Modern drugs continue to derive from prehistoric herbal knowledge, including artemisinin, digitalis, quinine, and taxanes.

## History & Development

- **Paleolithic (~60,000 years ago):** Earliest evidence of medicinal plant use; herbs found in Shanidar Cave; areca nut remains in Spirit Cave, Thailand
- **Prehistoric (~6,000 BCE):** Psychoactive plants (psilocybin mushrooms) used in the Sahara; plant-based treatments passed down orally
- **Sumerians (c. 3000 BCE):** First written records of herbal prescriptions on cuneiform tablets
- **Egyptians (c. 1600–1550 BCE):** Edwin Smith Papyrus (c. 1600 BCE) — earliest surgical text; Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BC) records 700+ compounds
- **India (1st millennium BC):** Ayurveda — Charaka and Sushruta described herbs and minerals
- **China (Han dynasty):** Shennong Bencaojing (Divine Farmer's Herb-Root Classic); Mawangdui manuscript (168 BC) lists 52 ailment recipes
- **Greece/Rome (c. 40–90 CE):** Dioscorides' De Materia Medica — 600+ plants; basis for medieval European texts

## Function & Use

- **Primary Use:** Treatment of diseases, ailments, and injuries using herbal, surgical, and spiritual methods
- **Secondary Uses:** Prevention of disease; spiritual healing and ritual practices; pain management; dental care; wound treatment
- **Cultural Significance:** Traditional medicine served as primary healthcare for millennia; healing blended physical and spiritual elements

## Construction & Technique

- **Herbal medicine:** Plant materials primary treatment; herbs prepared as decoctions, poultices, or ingested directly
- **Surgical techniques:** Trepanning — oldest surgical procedure (7,000–10,000 years ago); over 1,500 trephined skulls from Neolithic period
- **Dental care:** Earliest drilled and filled tooth (13,000 years ago, Italy); Mehrgarh (Pakistan, c. 7000 BCE) shows dental drilling evidence
- **Bone setting:** Clay applied to broken bones; ant pincers used in the Americas to close wounds
- **Wound care:** Geophagy — eating earth/clays for internal and external wound treatment
- **Pharmacological preparation:** Sumerians first to record herbal prescriptions; Egyptians documented 700+ compounds

## Regional Variations

- **Sumeria:** First written herbal prescriptions on cuneiform tablets (c. 3000 BCE)
- **Egypt:** Ebers Papyrus (700+ compounds, plant-based); Edwin Smith Papyrus (earliest surgical text)
- **India:** Ayurveda — Charaka and Sushruta (1st millennium BC); diet-based treatment, herbal remedies
- **China:** Shennong Bencaojing (earliest Chinese materia medica, Han dynasty); Mawangdui manuscript (52 ailment recipes)
- **Greece/Rome:** Dioscorides' De Materia Medica (600+ plants); basis for medieval European texts
- **Northern Europe:** Birch polypore fungus used as laxative; found in Ötzi the Iceman's possessions (c. 3230 BC)
- **Peru/Andes:** Highest concentration of trepanning; sophisticated techniques avoiding the midline; cranioplasty existed
- **Mesoamerica:** Maya abrasive grinding for trepanning; Aztec ethnomedicinal knowledge compiled by Sahagún
- **Africa:** Medicine men central to tribal systems; trepanning in East Africa (Bunyoro-Kitara, Kisii people)
- **Australia:** Bush medicine; Aboriginal plant use documented
- **Americas (Native American):** Navajo, Chumash, Mapuche medicine; herbal remedies passed down orally

## Uncertainties & Research Questions

- The exact nature of many prehistoric medicines remains unknown due to lack of preservation
- Cross-regional comparisons require more detailed analysis
- Ethnographic parallels need verification
- The extent of shamanic influence on early pharmacological development remains unclear
- Connections between prehistoric herbal knowledge and modern drug derivation require further study
